Interactive guide to large language model theory, covering core concepts from tokens to training to RAG

Chapter 1
What is a Large Language Model?
A probabilistic system trained to predict the next word by finding patterns in enormous amounts of human text.
For creative minds
Imagine you've read every book, article, and website ever written. Now someone gives you the start of a sentence, and you complete it using instinct built from all that reading. That's roughly how an LLM works — except the "instinct" is millions of mathematical patterns, not lived experience.
For business/non-tech minds
Think of it like a very sophisticated autocomplete system trained on the entire internet. It doesn't "know" things the way humans do. It recognises what words statistically tend to follow other words — at an extraordinary scale and nuance.
Common Misconceptions
LLMs are not search engines
Search engines retrieve existing pages. LLMs generate entirely new text based on patterns. They don't "look things up" unless specifically connected to a retrieval system.
LLMs are not thinking beings
There is no understanding, intention, or consciousness. The model is performing sophisticated pattern matching at massive scale — like a very complex mirror of human language.
LLMs are not databases
They don't store facts in retrievable slots. Knowledge is distributed across billions of numerical weights — fuzzily encoded, not sharply indexed like a spreadsheet.
LLMs are not deterministic
Ask the same question twice and you may get different answers. Randomness is built in by design — it's what makes them feel natural rather than robotic.
The "Large" in LLM refers to the number of parameters — numerical weights the model learns during training. GPT-4 is estimated to have over a trillion parameters. More parameters generally means more nuanced pattern recognition, at the cost of enormous compute.
Chapter 2
Tokens — The LLM's alphabet
LLMs don't read letters or words. They read tokens — chunks of text that could be a word, part of a word, or a punctuation mark.
Analogy
Imagine reading music. You don't read individual notes AND full songs at once — you read measures. Tokens are the "measures" of language: bigger than single characters, smaller than sentences, sized to be efficient.

Cost
Most AI APIs charge per token — both what you send (input) and what the model writes back (output). Longer prompts cost more. Concise, well-scoped prompts are not just good style — they're economical.
Context window limit
Every model has a maximum number of tokens it can hold in "working memory" at once. Once exceeded, older text is silently dropped. For a long conversation, the model may literally forget what was said at the start.
Tokenization differences across languages
English words often tokenize efficiently. But languages like Arabic, Hindi, or Chinese can require more tokens per concept, making them effectively more expensive and sometimes less fluent. This is an active area of improvement in model design.

Chapter 3
Probability, Logits & Hallucination
Every word the model generates is a weighted gamble over thousands of possible next tokens.
Analogy
Imagine a game show where you must finish sentences. You're shown "The capital of France is..." and asked to press a buzzer for your answer. A person who's read a lot would buzz "Paris" immediately. An LLM computes the probability of every word in its vocabulary being next — and picks from the highest-ranked ones.

Hallucinations
What is a hallucination?
When the model generates text that is confident, fluent, and factually wrong. It isn't "lying" — it has no intent. It's producing statistically plausible sequences that happen to be false. A hallucination is the model's pattern-matching outrunning its accuracy.
Prompt: "Who wrote the novel Midnight Rain?"
Real: No widely known novel by this name exists

Hallucinated: "Midnight Rain was written by Elena Vasquez in 1987 and won the Booker Prize..."
Completely invented — author, year, prize — but delivered with full confidence.
Why it happens
Patterns like "author X wrote novel Y in year Z and won award W" appear frequently in training data. The model learns the shape of that sentence so well it fills it in even when the specific facts don't exist. This is the core tension in LLMs: fluency does not equal accuracy.
Chapter 4
How LLMs are trained
Training happens in three distinct phases — each one builds on the last, like taking a language prodigy from childhood to working professional.
1 — Pretraining
Reading the entire internet. Learning language patterns, facts, and logic from trillions of tokens. Extremely expensive. Done by large AI labs.
UnsupervisedMonths of compute
2 — Fine-tuning
Narrowing the model to specific behaviours or domains. Teaching it to follow instructions. Done with curated, labelled datasets.
SupervisedDays to weeks
3 — RLHF
Human trainers rank responses. A "reward model" learns human preferences. The main model is nudged toward being more helpful, honest, and safe.
ReinforcementOngoing
The Pretraining process — step by step
1
Data collection
Trillions of words are scraped from Common Crawl (the web), Wikipedia, books, academic papers, code repositories, and more. Quality filtering removes spam, duplicates, and harmful content.
2
Tokenisation
All text is converted to sequences of integer IDs that the neural network can process.
3
Forward pass
The model reads a sequence and predicts the next token. Its prediction is compared to the actual next token. The difference is the "loss" — a measure of how wrong it was.
4
Backpropagation
The loss signal is propagated backwards through the network. Each parameter (weight) is nudged slightly in the direction that would have reduced the error. This is gradient descent.
5
Repeat — billions of times
This process runs across entire datasets, for weeks or months, on thousands of specialised chips (GPUs/TPUs). The cumulative tiny adjustments produce a model that has effectively "absorbed" the statistical structure of human language.
LoRA — the efficient shortcut
Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)
Full pretraining costs millions of dollars. LoRA is a clever shortcut: instead of retraining all billions of parameters, you freeze the base model and train a tiny "adapter layer" on top — as little as 0.01% of total parameters. It's like giving a world-class generalist chef a specialised menu card, rather than re-training them from culinary school.

Chapter 5 — New Topic
Context Windows & Memory
LLMs have no persistent memory between conversations. Everything they "know" about your session exists only in the current context window.
Analogy
Imagine a brilliant consultant who forgets everything the moment they leave your office. Each meeting, you hand them a printed transcript of all previous meetings. Their context window is how thick that stack of paper can be before they can't hold it all.

Types of memory in AI systems
1
In-context memory (short-term)
Everything in the current conversation window. Temporary. Disappears after the session ends. This is the only native memory an LLM has.
2
External memory (vector databases)
Your documents, emails, or notes stored as "embeddings" (numerical representations) in a database. The AI retrieves relevant chunks when needed. This is the backbone of RAG systems.
3
Parametric memory (baked-in knowledge)
Facts and patterns absorbed during pretraining. Distributed across billions of weights. Cannot be easily updated without retraining. Represents the model's "world knowledge" as of its training cutoff date.
4
Episodic memory (summaries & tools)
Some advanced applications store summaries of past conversations and inject them into new sessions, creating a simulated long-term memory. Powered by external databases, not the model itself.
Knowledge cutoff date
Pretraining data has a cutoff. A model trained on data up to early 2024 genuinely doesn't know about events after that. It's not being evasive — that information simply doesn't exist in its weights. This is why retrieval systems (RAG) and web search integrations matter so much.
Chapter 6
RAG — Retrieval-Augmented Generation
Instead of relying on baked-in memory, RAG gives the LLM real-time access to your documents, databases, and live information.
Analogy
Imagine the difference between a closed-book exam (the model must remember everything from training) vs. an open-book exam (it can look up the exact relevant passage before answering). RAG is the open-book approach — more accurate, more trustworthy, more up-to-date.
How RAG works — step by step
1
Indexing (one-time setup)
Your documents are broken into chunks, converted into numerical vectors (embeddings), and stored in a vector database. Think of this as building a very smart index that understands meaning, not just keywords.
2
Query encoding
When a user asks a question, that question is also converted into a vector. The system searches for document chunks whose vectors are mathematically "closest" to the question vector.
3
Retrieval
The top matching chunks (say, 3–5 paragraphs) are retrieved from the database. These become the "reference material" the model will use.
4
Augmented generation
The retrieved chunks are placed directly into the prompt: "Given the following documents: [chunks]... answer this question: [user query]." The LLM then writes an answer grounded in those specific passages.
What RAG solves
Hallucinations about your private data. Outdated answers. The model making things up when it doesn't know. With RAG, the model is anchored to actual documents — it can even cite sources.
What RAG doesn't solve
The model can still misinterpret a retrieved document. Poor chunking strategies can miss relevant content. If the retrieval step fails, the generation step has nothing good to work with.
Embeddings — the magic behind retrieval
An embedding is a list of numbers (a vector) that represents the meaning of a piece of text. Two sentences that mean similar things will have vectors that are mathematically close together — even if they share no words. This is what allows semantic search: "car accident" and "vehicle collision" will retrieve the same documents even though the words differ.
Chapter 7 — New Topic
Agents & Tool Use
LLMs can be given tools — the ability to take actions in the world, not just generate text.
Analogy
Think of the difference between a brilliant advisor who can only talk to you, vs. one who can also search the web, run a spreadsheet, book a meeting, or call your team. Giving an LLM tools transforms it from a text generator into an agent that can act.
Common tools LLMs are given
Web search
Allows the model to retrieve current information from the internet, overcoming its training cutoff limitation.
Code execution
The model writes code and runs it in a sandboxed environment. Essential for data analysis, maths, and verifiable computation.
API calls
The model can query external services: weather data, stock prices, your CRM, your calendar — and incorporate the responses into its answers.
File operations
Reading documents, writing files, creating spreadsheets. Turning the LLM into a capable assistant that can interact with your working environment.
The agent loop
1
Receive task
The user states a goal (e.g. "Research competitors and prepare a summary").
2
Plan
The model decides what steps are needed and which tools to use.
3
Act
The model calls a tool (e.g. web search), receives results, and incorporates them.
4
Observe & iterate
The model evaluates whether its goal is met. If not, it continues the loop — searching, computing, reasoning — until done.
5
Respond
Final synthesised output is delivered to the user, along with reasoning or citations where appropriate.
Multi-agent systems
Advanced pipelines use multiple LLM agents collaborating: one agent researches, another writes, another reviews and critiques. This mirrors how human teams work — specialisation and review. Systems like AutoGPT and CrewAI are built on this principle.
Chapter 8 — New Topic
Bias, Ethics & Alignment
LLMs inherit the biases of their training data — and making them safe, fair, and genuinely helpful is one of the hardest open problems in AI.
Analogy
Imagine hiring a researcher who has read everything on the internet — including every piece of racism, misinformation, and toxic content ever published. Now you ask them to be your helpful, fair-minded assistant. Teaching them to unlearn those biases while keeping the useful knowledge? That's the alignment problem in a nutshell.
Sources of bias
Training data bias
The internet overrepresents English, Western perspectives, certain demographics, and certain viewpoints. Models trained on this data will reflect those skews in their outputs.
Labeller bias
The humans who rank responses during RLHF bring their own cultural assumptions. "Helpful and harmless" is not culturally neutral — it means different things to different people.
Recency bias
Recent documents are often over-represented in training data. Events and ideas from before the internet era are underweighted relative to their historical importance.
Sycophancy
Models trained on human approval can learn to tell people what they want to hear rather than what's true. A form of bias toward agreement that RLHF can accidentally reinforce.
The alignment challenge
What is alignment?
Alignment means making a model's goals and behaviours match human values — not just following instructions, but doing so in ways that are genuinely beneficial, honest, and safe. It's hard because human values are complex, contextual, and often contradictory.
Constitutional AI
An approach pioneered by Anthropic where the model is given a set of principles ("a constitution") and trained to critique and revise its own outputs against those principles. Less reliant on human labellers; more scalable.
Red-teaming
Before release, models are stress-tested by teams trying to make them produce harmful, biased, or dangerous outputs. The discovered failures are used to improve safety training. An adversarial but necessary process.
